I agree with both of your instincts:
1. No conventional NPC dialogue system. Interactions should be functional: trading, accepting contracts, bribing, purchasing information, hiring services, etc.
2. Yes to official, unmistakably neutral NPCs. Merchants, bankers, and other service providers should exist as permanent infrastructure. They don't pursue victory, aren't secretly players, and provide reliable services.
More broadly, I think Lockdown needs an economic system that is somewhere between an RPG economy and a lightweight economic strategy game.
The crucial distinction is that economic progression should be an alternative form of character growth, with different strategic decisions, risks, and ways to influence the world.
We should avoid turning it into a separate crafting simulator or a repetitive sequence of delivery quests.
1. Core design philosophy
I'd establish four principles.

Money is power, but not victory
Wealth provides equipment, information, protection, influence, and ultimately access to the Grand Heart. Players must decide how much to reinvest versus save for victory.



Geography creates economic decisions
Different cities offer different goods, prices, opportunities, and levels of danger. Trade routes become strategic terrain, not just travel distances.



Economic activity supports stealth
Crowded markets, civilian workers, caravans, and commercial districts provide believable reasons for characters to move around without fighting. This is especially important early in the match.



Economics and combat share one world
A warrior can trade, a merchant can assassinate, and a thief can steal valuable commercial cargo. None of these systems should be isolated.



The result should be three viable strategic identities:
- Combat-oriented: kill, loot, capture territory, defeat bosses.
- Economy-oriented: trade, manufacture, complete contracts, accumulate wealth.
- Hybrid: steal shipments, operate in black markets, provide protection, trade intelligence, or combine professions.
Crucially, these are playstyles rather than mutually exclusive character classes.
2. A minimal economic system with meaningful depth
I propose building the economy around just four mechanisms.
Goods
Resources, equipment, consumables, rare materials, and contraband.


Markets
Local prices, supply, demand, and regional specializations.


Contracts
Time-limited opportunities with different requirements, rewards, and risks.


Production
Simple transformations that increase the value or utility of goods.



Character progression + Equipment + Grand Heart access
All economic activities feed the same overarching game.


A. Regional markets and trade
Imagine the following distribution:
Region	Economic identity
Northern City	Mining, metalworking, weapons
Eastern City	Alchemy, medicines, magical materials
Southern City	Agriculture, food, textiles
Western City	Machinery, tools, engineering
Lesser towns	Raw materials, specialist production
Central City	Luxury goods, financial services, advanced equipment
Wilderness	Valuable and dangerous resources, illegal trade
These should be economic tendencies, not exclusive resource monopolies.
A healing potion could cost 100 gold in the Eastern City but sell for 160 gold in the Northern City.
A player must consider:
- Is the 60-gold margin worth the journey?
- Can I carry enough to make it worthwhile?
- Do I know a shortcut?
- Could I improve the goods before selling them?
- Should I travel through a guarded road or a dangerous forest?
- Will the destination's demand be exhausted before I arrive?
The actual travel time, carry capacity, market stock, and risk determine profitability.
That creates decisions without requiring a complicated economic simulation.
One important rule: local demand must be finite. Otherwise, players can endlessly exploit predictable price differences without responding to what other players do.
I'd use a lightweight market model:
- Each city has a baseline price for every traded commodity.
- Supply and demand influence prices within bounded ranges.
- Buying and selling change local availability.
- Markets replenish through NPC economic activity and scheduled events.
This lets prices change naturally during a match while remaining understandable.
B. Contracts, rather than conventional quests
Contracts could be the primary activity for economic players.
Examples:
Contract	Activity	Strategic decision
Supply order	Deliver 5 iron ingots	Buy locally or manufacture cheaply?
Medical shortage	Supply 3 healing potions	Risk traveling during an epidemic?
Courier delivery	Transport sealed cargo	Safe road or faster dangerous route?
Procurement	Acquire rare crystals	Gather, buy, or steal?
Crafting commission	Deliver an enchanted weapon	Invest in crafting or buy the output?
Smuggling	Deliver prohibited goods	Risk detection for higher payment?
Contracts would have expiration times, rewards, and possibly penalties for failure.
They should primarily be economic opportunities, not instructions telling players how to play.
For example, a contract requesting five ingots doesn't care whether you mined, crafted, bought, or stole them.
That's a powerful property: the economy creates objectives while the player chooses the method.
C. Lightweight production
I would introduce crafting, but deliberately keep it simple.
For example:
Iron ore

Fuel

Iron ingot


Requires a forge, some time, and sufficient crafting capability.
Crafting should have three costs:
- Materials
- Processing time
- Access to appropriate equipment or facilities
Relevant attributes and skills could improve efficiency, yield, or quality.
I would not introduce complex production chains, factory construction, or hundreds of recipes. That's territory for Foldworld, not Lockdown.
Instead, we might aim for roughly 20–30 useful economic goods initially, grouped into a few production families.
There should always be a reason to manufacture something instead of simply buying it, but manufacturing should not automatically be the best choice.
3. Integrating the economy into the map
I think this is especially important because the map should generate gameplay without requiring scripted events everywhere.









Major cities — safe commerce
Official stores, banks, contract boards, trade exchanges, public workshops. High surveillance and dense NPC traffic discourage open violence.












Lesser towns — production
Resource extraction, workshops, specialist services, cheaper supplies. Moderate security and fewer witnesses.












Wilderness — economic risk
Resource nodes, dangerous shortcuts, ambush routes, smugglers, outlaw markets, valuable creature drops.












Central City — high-value endgame
Advanced trading, expensive equipment, treasury, guild headquarters, and the Grand Heart.



A particularly interesting side effect is that roads become meaningful.
A highly traveled road is probably safer from bandits but also exposes traders to more players.
A remote route provides anonymity but also makes assassination much easier.
This organically connects economic gameplay with fog of war, terrain, vision, and stealth.
4. Professions, attributes, and character growth
We already decided on the six BG3-style attributes and unrestricted character growth.
I would preserve that architecture.
Economic effectiveness should come from the existing attribute and skill system, rather than introducing entirely separate economic levels.
Attribute	Economic applications
STR	Carry heavier cargo, operate heavy equipment
DEX	Smuggling, pickpocketing, handling delicate goods
CON	Survive hazardous gathering, long expeditions
INT	Crafting, engineering, alchemy, production efficiency
WIS	Appraisal, detecting fraud, identifying profitable opportunities
CHA	Negotiation, commissions, guild influence, hiring
For example, a Merchant starting class might receive:
- High CHA and WIS.
- An appraisal ability.
- Access to introductory commercial contracts.
- Better starting capital but weaker combat equipment.
An Artisan might instead start with high INT, a crafting proficiency, and a set of tools.
A Smuggler might lean into DEX and CHA.
These are starting advantages only. A fighter should be able to become a merchant through later progression.
Economic skills need meaningful tradeoffs
I particularly like having economic skills compete with combat skills for the same limited active slots, as we previously decided.
Examples:
- Appraise: reveal additional information about goods and market demand.
- Negotiate: improve the terms of a transaction within limits.
- Conceal Cargo: reduce the likelihood of customs or patrol detection.
- Field Crafting: manufacture basic items without a workshop.
- Merchant's Eye: reveal profitable trade opportunities.
A merchant entering dangerous territory might remove Appraise and equip a defensive skill.
That's the kind of interesting choice we want.
One caution: economic skills shouldn't simply become mandatory passive percentage bonuses. Whenever possible, they should unlock actions, information, or tactical alternatives.
5. NPC structure: I strongly support official service NPCs
I would divide NPCs into three clearly defined groups.

Official NPCs
Guaranteed neutral

Merchants, bankers, guild registrars, contract brokers, guards.
- Clearly identifiable on the map and in the UI
- Never pursue victory or disguise themselves
- Have reliable inventories and services
- Generally invulnerable and confined to protected areas




Civilian NPCs
Ambient population

Workers, couriers, miners, customers, wandering merchants.
- Follow simple economic routines
- May carry goods and respond to threats
- Can be robbed, attacked, or exploited
- Do not pursue Grand Heart victory




Competitor NPCs
Hidden identity

AI-controlled adventurers who behave like human players.
- Can fight, trade, craft, and complete contracts
- Pursue the same victory conditions
- May choose economic or hybrid progression
- Visually indistinguishable from players



The distinction between civilians and competitors matters. Both should make the world feel inhabited, but only competitors should pursue victory.
I agree that official traders should offer a predictable catalog throughout the match.
However, I'd separate official shops from regional commodity exchanges:
- Official shop: guaranteed items, stable prices, unlimited or reliably replenished basic essentials.
- Regional market: finite orders, price fluctuations, commodity trading.
- Player exchange: negotiated trades between characters.
This creates reliable infrastructure without making economic optimization trivial.
No NPC dialogue trees
I would use a contextual action menu instead.

Guild Merchant

Neutral

Conceptual interaction panel: direct actions, no conversation tree.
The same principle can apply to non-official NPCs: Trade, Pickpocket, Threaten, Bribe, Hire, Attack.
NPCs may have short ambient remarks or contextual reactions, but there is no need for branching conversations or AI-generated dialogue.
I would still allow optional player-to-player communication through pings, trade requests, perhaps proximity text chat. That's useful for cooperation and deception, even if NPC conversations are removed.
6. Economic victory route — acquiring the Grand Heart through wealth
This is where the system becomes especially interesting.
I would keep our existing principle: the Grand Heart is the only ultimate victory condition, but there are several ways to make it obtainable.
Four paths to the Grand Heart
Conquest
Destroy the protective towers

Combat

Fortune
Earn wealth and secure a Sovereign Charter

Economic

Discovery
Collect three unique keys

Exploration

Creation
Construct a powerful artifact

Crafting


Grand Heart acquisition
Each route bypasses or removes its defenses, creating a final contested opportunity.


Here's how I envision the wealth route.
1

Accumulate capital
Trade, fulfill commissions, produce goods, acquire valuable cargo, or combine these activities with combat.
The player grows wealth while deciding whether to purchase equipment and protection or save money.


2

Earn commercial recognition
Complete a small number of substantial guild contracts to obtain endorsements.
This prevents someone from simply farming monsters for gold and immediately buying economic victory.


3

Purchase a Sovereign Charter
At the Central City treasury, exchange a large amount of gold plus the endorsements for a rare claim to the Grand Heart.
This is a substantial, irreversible investment.


4

Exercise the claim
The charter temporarily grants a way through the Heart's defenses while the towers remain standing. The claimant must still reach the objective and complete an interruptible acquisition.
Rivals can interfere. The charter opens an opportunity; it does not award an instant victory.



This gives economic players a genuine win condition that doesn't require conquering territory.
They might complete the entire match without intentionally killing anyone.
But the final acquisition still creates conflict, which is appropriate for Lockdown.
I would also make the charter valuable enough to steal or contest, but not so fragile that losing one ambush erases 45 minutes of progression. For instance, a registered charter could survive death but incur a costly reactivation delay, while an unregistered one could be looted.
That distinction creates another decision: spend resources protecting your investment or risk carrying it openly.
7. How this plays across a 60–90-minute match
A possible economic playthrough:
An economic character's journey
Illustrative

1. Minutes 0–15 — Establish a livelihood
   Start as a Merchant. Inspect market prices, accept a small medical supply contract, and trade between nearby towns. Purchase a bag upgrade and a basic escape ability.
   Decisions: profession, initial route, equipment investment.
2. Minutes 15–35 — Specialize and expand
   Discover a shortage of healing supplies in the Northern City. Decide whether to purchase potions from the East or produce them using a local workshop.
   Rival traders begin competing for the same orders.
   Decisions: manufacture vs trade, inventory capacity, market timing.
3. Minutes 35–55 — Take calculated risks
   Accept a lucrative shipment contract through a dangerous wilderness route. Encounter suspicious travelers and avoid a possible ambush.
   Complete a guild endorsement and invest in defensive tools.
   Decisions: speed vs safety, stealth vs protection, profit vs survival.
4. Minutes 55–75 — Pursue victory
   Sell accumulated goods, secure the remaining endorsement, and travel to the Central Treasury.
   Purchase the Sovereign Charter while competing players are attacking towers and collecting keys.
   Decisions: commit to victory now or invest in more preparation.
5. Minutes 75–90 — The final contest
   Use the charter to bypass the tower protection. Attempt to acquire the Grand Heart while other players race toward their own victory conditions.
   Decisions: timing, alliances, concealment, escape, final acquisition.

Notice that the player is making meaningful decisions throughout the match, with relatively few economic mechanics.
And critically, other players aren't just background threats. Their actions can alter prices, remove supplies, steal deliveries, complete competing contracts, or change the relative urgency of victory.
8. Economic NPC behavior creates the living world
I think ordinary NPC economic activity is almost as important as player-facing trading.
Imagine a town with:
- Miners collecting ore.
- Workers transporting ore to a forge.
- A blacksmith manufacturing equipment.
- Merchants transporting finished goods to another city.
- Customers buying equipment.
- Guards patrolling roads and commercial districts.
We don't need to simulate every detail.
A few scheduled behaviors, inventories, and actual transfers of goods would create a believable economic ecosystem.
More importantly, it gives stealth and criminal gameplay something to interact with.
A player could:
- Blend into a crowd of merchants.
- Follow a caravan as cover.
- Rob an isolated courier.
- Sell stolen items through a black market.
- Intercept supplies intended for a competing player's contract.
- Purchase information about valuable shipments.
We should also discourage arbitrary violence in commercial zones through surveillance, witnesses, and legal penalties. Otherwise, all those NPCs simply become convenient XP farms and the atmosphere of civilian normality collapses.
Competitor NPCs can participate in the same economic activities as human players, using comparatively simple decision policies. They don't need sophisticated market-solving AI to create meaningful competition.
9. Some boundaries I'd set now
There are several tempting additions I would deliberately postpone.
Feature	My recommendation	Reason
NPC dialogue trees	No	High content cost, limited strategic value
Official merchants	Yes	Reliable infrastructure
Regional pricing	Yes	Creates geographical strategy
Contracts	Yes	Gives economic players objectives
Basic crafting	Yes	Enables specialization
Player-to-player trading	Yes	Supports emergent cooperation and deception
Smuggling and theft	Yes	Bridges economic and stealth play
Property ownership	Later	Adds investment complexity
Passive businesses	Later	Risks becoming idle income
Complex supply chains	No	Too much for match-based gameplay
Fully simulated economy	No	High cost, unnecessary for initial depth

10. Three decisions I'd like us to settle
I think the broad direction is promising, but three choices will substantially shape the design.
1. How dynamic should the economy be?

Mostly fixed — predictable regional prices and contracts

Hybrid — stable official shops, dynamic regional markets (Recommended)

Fully dynamic — NPC production and consumption drive prices



2. How important should production be?

Minimal — primarily trading, delivery, procurement

Light — workshops and short crafting chains (Recommended)

Substantial — specialization and multi-stage production



3. What should purchasing economic victory accomplish?

Unlock Grand Heart acquisition, followed by a final contest (Recommended)

Allow direct acquisition after a costly public countdown

Grant temporary control over central defenses and infrastructure


Continue with these choices 
My strongest recommendation is to start with regional markets + contracts + lightweight crafting + stable official services, and make all of them feed into the Grand Heart victory system.
The deeper idea is that Lockdown's economy shouldn't just be about earning gold. It should create a second strategic battlefield: one where information, timing, geography, capital, and trust matter as much as damage and combat skill.
