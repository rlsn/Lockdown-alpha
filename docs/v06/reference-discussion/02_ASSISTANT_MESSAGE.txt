I think these three decisions give Lockdown a solid economic foundation. We can now design something relatively simple mechanically, but strategically rich because it interacts with the rest of the game.

My proposed direction is to treat the economy as a second progression system that competes with combat for time, resources, and map control.

A combat character becomes stronger by fighting. An economic character becomes stronger by accumulating capital, acquiring capabilities, exploiting opportunities, and eventually converting wealth into access to the Grand Heart.

Both are participating in the same match.

## 1. The economic gameplay loop

I'd organize economic progression into three stages.

Earn

Trade, craft, contracts, loot

Reinvest

Equipment, skills, capacity, information

Claim

Guild recognition, charter, Grand Heart

The critical decision is whether to spend or save.

Suppose a merchant has 1,000 gold:

- Buy more cargo to increase future profits.
- Purchase a powerful defensive item to reduce assassination risk.
- Upgrade their carrying capacity.
- Learn a useful skill.
- Save toward the Sovereign Charter.

This creates an interesting distinction between wealth and actual power.

An economically successful player might be very rich but poorly equipped, making them an attractive assassination target.

Conversely, a player who spends everything on equipment might survive easily but struggle to finance their victory condition.

I wouldn't introduce a separate economic XP system. Gold, equipment, skills, attributes, and guild endorsements should provide sufficient progression.

## 2. Market mechanics: simple underlying rules, emergent outcomes

Let's make the hybrid market concrete.

Each region has a market inventory, baseline prices, and current demand.

For each commodity, we can model:

\\[ P = P\_{\text{base}}\times M\_{\text{regional}}\times M\_{\text{scarcity}}\times M\_{\text{event}} \\]

We can bound the modifiers to keep prices understandable.

For example:

The player sees an opportunity, but pursuing it has consequences:

- Purchasing ten potions raises the Eastern market price.
- Selling ten potions reduces Northern prices as its demand is filled.
- Other traders may notice the same opportunity.
- The journey costs time and exposes the player to danger.

This produces natural competition without simulating an entire economy.

### How markets replenish

I'd use simplified background economic activity:

- Civilian NPCs generate resources through their occupations.
- Trade caravans transfer goods between regions.
- Local consumption gradually removes goods.
- Random regional events alter production and demand.

We could simulate most of this statistically, only spawning actual NPC movements near players.

One important exploit-prevention rule: official shops always provide essential goods and services, but they should never offer an unlimited profitable buyback loop. Regional markets have finite liquidity and demand, so players cannot print infinite gold by repeatedly crafting or reselling the same goods.

## 3. Light crafting should support both professions and combat

I favor very short production chains: normally one transformation, occasionally two.

I would make workshops fixed map facilities initially, not player-buildable factories.

The more interesting connection is that crafted products enter the same equipment and quick-slot systems as combat loot.

A warrior might rely on a merchant to provide specialized equipment. A merchant might craft a defensive artifact for personal protection.

We also get natural cooperation between classes without requiring formal parties.

## 4. Economic growth should alter what players can do

Merely increasing earning rates would become boring.

Instead, economic growth should expand the player's strategic possibilities.

| Progression                | New capability                                                   |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Better Appraisal           | Identify valuable goods, shortages, and hidden properties        |
| Higher CHA                 | Negotiate better contracts and access more lucrative commissions |
| Higher INT                 | Manufacture advanced products or use materials more efficiently  |
| Better logistics equipment | Carry larger volumes with fewer trips                            |
| Guild endorsements         | Access high-value contracts and the Sovereign Charter            |
| Wealth                     | Buy rare equipment, hire services, fund victory                  |

I'd also make market knowledge imperfect.

Players visiting a market see current prices, but information about distant cities might be outdated.

A character with Appraisal or commercial contacts could obtain more accurate information.

That creates an actual information advantage, and makes the economic profession meaningfully different from ordinary trading.

## 5. Economic competition should be able to become violent

Here's a particularly important principle: economic success should generate conflict without forcing economic players to initiate combat.

Consider this scenario:

1. The Northern City experiences a shortage of medical supplies.
2. Several players recognize the opportunity.
3. One merchant purchases a large shipment.
4. A competitor sees the merchant leave the Eastern City.
5. The competitor decides whether to race them, find another market, or attempt an ambush.
6. The merchant chooses a guarded road, a concealed wilderness path, or purchases an escort service.

Now we have trading, information, combat, stealth, and geography interacting through a single economic event.

This is exactly the kind of emergent gameplay I think Lockdown needs.

### Crime and protected areas

I suggest three security environments:

| Zone            | Violence                                     | Economic characteristics                           |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Major cities    | Possible, but heavily punished if witnessed  | Banks, contracts, official stores, expensive goods |
| Towns and roads | Moderate risk; patrols and witnesses         | Workshops, trade routes, local markets             |
| Wilderness      | Few legal consequences, high personal danger | Rare resources, smuggling, ambush opportunities    |

No strict PvP-disabled zones are necessary except possibly tiny protected interaction spaces around essential official services.

An assassin can attempt a murder in a marketplace. They simply need to consider guards, witnesses, escape routes, and their resulting wanted status.

That's more interesting than absolute safe zones.

## 6. Economic victory: a public acquisition window

Now I'd refine the Sovereign Charter mechanic.

The tricky question is: what exactly does the Charter do to the Grand Heart?

If it only protects the holder from towers, an economic player might gain an almost uncontestable win.

If it completely removes the defenses permanently, the economic player pays the entire price while competitors receive the same benefit for free.

My recommendation is a limited, publicly announced acquisition window.

## Sovereign Charter activation

Proposed

1. Purchase

   Accumulate sufficient gold and two commercial endorsements. Purchase the Charter from the Central Treasury.
2. Activate

   Initiate the acquisition procedure at a civic terminal. A global warning announces the impending opening of the Heart.
3. Challenge period

   After approximately 60 seconds, one guarded approach to the Grand Heart becomes accessible for a limited time. Other players can contest this route.
4. Acquire

   The Heart becomes capturable for approximately 120 seconds. The Charter holder may attempt the established 10-second acquisition, but enemies can interrupt or capture it themselves.

Balance principle

The economic player purchases control over when a temporary Grand Heart contest begins—not exclusive ownership of its outcome.

That would be a major strategic advantage.

The economic player can prepare equipment, position themselves, choose an opportune moment, and surprise competitors who aren't ready.

But there's still a final confrontation.

The alternative routes could create different types of openings:

| Route      | Grand Heart access                    | Strategic advantage     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Conquest   | Permanently destroy towers            | Long-term map control   |
| Wealth     | Temporarily open a contested route    | Control the timing      |
| Three Keys | Secretly unlock a protected entrance  | Surprise and stealth    |
| Artifact   | Temporarily suppress magical defenses | Specialized preparation |

This gives each strategy a distinctive endgame rather than just four ways of paying the same entrance fee.

An important consequence: once any route opens the Grand Heart, everyone has a chance to win. That should help drive our desired late-game escalation from stealth into chaos.

## 7. The next decisions that matter most

I see four unresolved decisions, with my preferred defaults.

A. Cargo, banking, and death penalties

Should economic players risk losing everything they carry?

My recommendation:

- Carried commercial goods drop on death.
- Gold suffers the existing death penalty.
- Banks protect deposited wealth, but require visiting a physical location.
- Skills, attributes, and guild progress persist through death.

This gives merchants reasons to plan routes and secure wealth without making one death erase an entire match.

B. How contracts are allocated

Should every player be able to complete the same supply contract independently?

I recommend a mix:

- Small repeatable commissions for baseline income.
- Limited high-value regional contracts that disappear when fulfilled.
- Rare guild contracts that grant victory-related endorsements.

This creates economic competition while preserving accessible progression.

C. How much direct player-to-player trading

My recommendation is to support direct item-for-gold and item-for-item exchanges from the beginning, with an escrow-style confirmation screen.

Later, players could issue their own contracts and hire each other for services.

We don't need a global auction house initially. Geographic markets are more valuable to this design.

D. Whether economic facilities can be controlled

Could a player own a workshop, control a caravan route, or monopolize a market?

I would initially allow temporary use, not ownership.

Long-term property and passive income risk encouraging players to remain in safe areas collecting money.

Later, we could introduce temporary facility control if the economy needs more strategic depth.

## My biggest concern: economic gameplay must not become repetitive

The biggest risk isn't insufficient systems. It's that an economic player discovers one profitable loop and repeats it for 45 minutes.

To prevent this, we need a few forms of pressure:

- Diminishing opportunity: Profitable markets saturate as goods are delivered.
- Changing information: Events and other players create new opportunities.
- Increasing stakes: More capital means more valuable cargo and more expensive losses.
- Progression choices: Economic skills and equipment enable qualitatively different strategies.
- Victory pressure: The economic player must eventually convert wealth into a contested Grand Heart acquisition.

I think we can achieve meaningful depth with surprisingly few item types and production recipes if these pressures work properly.

My suggestion for our next discussion is to focus on economic risk, ownership, and logistics.

Specifically, I think we should decide whether merchants physically transport their goods in their regular character inventory, whether gold can be banked, and how theft, insurance, guards, and commercial cargo interact with our existing stealth and combat mechanics.

Those decisions will determine whether trading actually feels like a strategic playstyle rather than simply buying low and selling high.
