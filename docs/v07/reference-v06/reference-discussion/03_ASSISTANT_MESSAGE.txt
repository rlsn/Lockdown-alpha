I think we can now give Lockdown a fairly complete economic architecture.

My recommendation is a semi-closed, match-based economy: players generate wealth through activities, trade existing wealth and goods with one another, and remove wealth from circulation through purchases, services, penalties, and victory investments.

Unlike a persistent MMO economy, we don't need long-term equilibrium. We need an economy that evolves meaningfully over a 60–90-minute match, rewards different strategies, and ultimately pushes everyone toward the Grand Heart.

There are two tightly connected parts to design:

1. Microeconomy: what a player carries, risks, purchases, produces, and trades.
2. Macroeconomy: how gold, goods, and economic opportunities are created, circulated, and destroyed.

# 1. Logistics, inventory, and economic risk

## One unified inventory

I'd keep our existing large inventory plus limited quick-use slots.

All items—combat equipment, crafting materials, consumables, trade goods—should exist in the same inventory system.

However, we should introduce carrying capacity.

Equipment, attributes, and inventory upgrades determine capacity. Heavy loads can reduce movement speed and stealth effectiveness.

This creates several meaningful decisions.

A high-STR warrior might carry a large shipment without sacrificing mobility. A DEX-based smuggler might carry fewer goods but travel undetected.

A merchant can buy a larger backpack, but that comes at the opportunity cost of other equipment.

I would avoid introducing separate trade-cargo slots or vehicle inventories initially.

## Death and looting

We should preserve our existing respawn model, where death causes a time penalty and gold loss rather than permanent elimination.

My proposed economic rules:

| Asset                         | Upon death                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Carried trade commodities     | Dropped into a lootable container                          |
| Unique keys                   | Dropped, consistent with our earlier decision              |
| Equipped weapons and armor    | Retained                                                   |
| Learned skills and attributes | Retained                                                   |
| Guild endorsements            | Retained                                                   |
| Gold carried on person        | Partially lost or dropped                                  |
| Bank deposits                 | Protected                                                  |
| Sovereign Charter             | Temporarily deactivated; requires recovery or reactivation |

I'd make dropped cargo recoverable if the owner returns before another character loots it.

The key balancing principle is that death should hurt an economic character without erasing all their long-term progression.

## Banking

I strongly favor banks, but very simple ones.

- Official banks exist in major cities.
- Deposits are protected.
- Withdrawals and deposits require visiting a bank.
- No interest, borrowing, investments, or bank ownership initially.
- A modest transaction fee can act as a gold sink.

This adds an interesting tradeoff: players must decide how much money to carry into dangerous areas.

I'd also let players use their bank balance directly when purchasing a Sovereign Charter at the Central Treasury. The character must physically travel there, so secure banking doesn't eliminate the final geographical risk.

### Guards and insurance

For the first version:

- NPC road patrols provide environmental protection.
- Players can hire one temporary escort NPC for a fee.
- Player-to-player escort arrangements are possible through direct trade.
- No insurance system yet.

Insurance would introduce considerable complexity and could undermine the value of theft and ambushes.

# 2. The macroeconomic model

We should explicitly distinguish three kinds of economic transactions.

Sources

Create gold or items

Transfers

Change ownership

Sinks

Destroy gold or items

This distinction matters.

If Player A pays Player B 500 gold, no gold has been created. Wealth merely changes hands.

If a monster drops 500 gold generated by the game, 500 gold enters circulation.

If Player A purchases a 500-gold item from an official NPC that removes the payment from the system, 500 gold leaves circulation.

We need to balance the rates of these operations over a match.

## 3. Gold sources and sinks

### Gold sources — faucets

| Source                  | Mechanism                                         | Control                       |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Starting capital        | Each character begins with some gold              | Fixed per character           |
| PvE rewards             | Killing monsters and completing combat objectives | Spawn and reward budgets      |
| NPC contracts           | Deliver goods or complete tasks for payment       | Limited contract availability |
| NPC commodity purchases | Sell goods into official demand orders            | Purchase quotas and budgets   |
| World events            | Special objectives provide rewards                | Event frequency and payout    |
| Treasure                | Discover gold in the world                        | Limited spawning              |

These are the main ways new gold enters the player economy.

Combat and economic professions both need reliable income sources.

A combat character might earn money from monsters. An economic character might earn money by fulfilling a merchant's commission.

Neither should automatically dominate in gold earned per minute under comparable risk.

### Gold sinks

| Sink               | Mechanism                       | Purpose                            |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Official stores    | Buy equipment and consumables   | Remove gold; inject items          |
| Crafting fees      | Pay to use workshops            | Charge for production convenience  |
| Travel services    | Fast transport or hired escorts | Trade money for time or safety     |
| Banking fees       | Deposit/withdrawal services     | Minor convenience sink             |
| Market fees        | Small exchange commissions      | Reduce trivial trading loops       |
| Criminal penalties | Fines and confiscations         | Discourage indiscriminate violence |
| Death penalties    | Destroy a fraction of lost gold | Maintain risk                      |
| Sovereign Charter  | Large irreversible payment      | Major late-game gold sink          |

The Charter is particularly important.

It provides a natural endgame sink for accumulated economic wealth, analogous to the way combat consumes resources through powerful equipment and repeated conflicts.

### Gold transfers

- Player-to-player purchases.
- Robbery and looting.
- Bank deposits and withdrawals.
- Player bounties funded by other players.
- Trades against regional exchanges, when the exchange's gold reserve is explicitly tracked.

These do not inherently create or destroy gold, although transaction fees may be removed.

An important subtlety: a regional market purchase appears to remove gold from the player's wallet, but the money enters the market's treasury. Selling later draws money from that treasury.

That brings us to market liquidity.

# 4. Regional markets should hold both goods and gold

I'd give each regional exchange two tracked resources:

1. Commodity inventories.
2. Available purchasing liquidity.

Buy = price the exchange pays the player; Sell = price charged to the player. Figures are illustrative.

When a player sells items to the exchange:

- The market receives items.
- Its reserve pays gold to the player.
- Its stock increases.
- The commodity price tends to decrease.

When a player buys items:

- The market loses items.
- Its reserve receives gold.
- Its stock decreases.
- The price tends to increase.

This makes speculative hoarding, regional arbitrage, and market timing viable tactics.

However, unlimited sales are impossible if the exchange doesn't have sufficient liquidity or purchase orders.

### Background NPC economy

Every few minutes, the world processes simplified civilian economic activity:

- Local producers add certain goods to regional exchanges.
- Local consumers remove certain goods.
- NPC purchases inject gold into exchange reserves.
- Payments to abstract producers remove gold from those reserves.
- Caravans transfer stock between regions.

For the prototype, these can be aggregated transactions instead of individually simulated NPCs.

Visible caravans and workers can represent some of those transactions where players are nearby.

The aggregate model should remain authoritative so NPCs don't need to pathfind across the entire map for the economy to work.

# 5. Item sources and sinks

Gold is only half the picture.

Items need their own production, circulation, and destruction mechanisms.

| Item source        | Examples                                      |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Resource gathering | Ore, wood, plants, crystals                   |
| PvE drops          | Materials, equipment, consumables             |
| Official stores    | Basic weapons, tools, healing supplies        |
| NPC production     | Regional resource and commodity replenishment |
| World events       | Rare materials and special items              |
| Crafting outputs   | Potions, ingots, weapons, magical items       |

| Item sink            | Examples                                 |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Consumption          | Food, potions, scrolls, ammunition       |
| Crafting inputs      | Ore consumed when producing ingots       |
| Contract fulfillment | Delivered goods removed from circulation |
| Item disposal        | Destroying or discarding goods           |
| Expiration           | Perishable goods, if supported later     |
| Loot despawning      | Unrecovered items lost after death       |

Trade and robbery are transfers, not item sources.

Crafting is a transformation: it destroys input items and creates output items.

For an ordinary production chain:

\\[ \text{Ore} + \text{Fuel} \rightarrow \text{Ingot} \\]

The ingot may subsequently be converted into a weapon, consumed by a contract, or traded to another player.

### Why item sinks are necessary

Suppose players continually gather iron but rarely consume it.

Eventually, iron stock saturates regional exchanges and its price collapses.

That outcome can be interesting, but if it happens to every commodity, the economic game becomes stagnant.

Contracts are useful here because they create demand.

An NPC order for 50 iron ingots is effectively a controlled item sink that rewards players for manufacturing and delivering those goods.

Combat consumables provide a second source of demand.

This creates a useful feedback relationship:

- Combat produces loot and materials.
- Economic production converts materials into useful goods.
- Combat and contracts consume those goods.
- New gathering and production opportunities emerge.

Neither profession exists in isolation.

# 6. The economy should evolve throughout the match

Because Lockdown is match-based, I wouldn't try to maintain a perfectly balanced economy indefinitely.

I'd target three phases.

Early game — Capital formation

0–25 minutes

Gold enters the economy through starting funds, basic PvE, and entry-level contracts. Markets have healthy starting inventories. Players acquire equipment and choose specializations.

Midgame — Investment and competition

25–60 minutes

Wealth grows, regional shortages appear, specialized crafting becomes valuable, and high-value contracts attract competition. Large shipments and wealthy players become strategic targets.

Late game — Capital conversion

60–90 minutes

Wealthy players spend accumulated capital on the Charter, expensive equipment, and final preparations. Economic activity progressively gives way to Grand Heart acquisition.

We should expect player-held gold to rise initially, fluctuate during the midgame, and potentially decline sharply as victory investments are made.

That's fine.

The economy is supposed to lead toward an endgame, not remain permanently sustainable.

# 7. A minimal accounting model

For implementation, I'd track the following state.

World economy state

Gold

- Player and competitor-NPC wallets
- Player bank balances
- Regional market reserves

Goods

- Player inventories
- Regional market inventories
- Dropped world items
- Resource-node inventories

Economic opportunities

- Outstanding contracts
- Regional demand
- Local production rates
- Active market events

Let \\(G_t\\) be the total tracked gold across wallets, banks, and exchange reserves.

Then:

\\[ G\_{t+1}=G_t+F_t-S_t \\]

Where:

- \\(F_t\\) is gold newly created through rewards, initial grants, and externally funded NPC demand.
- \\(S_t\\) is gold destroyed through official services, purchases, penalties, and the Charter.

Transfers between tracked accounts cancel out.

We can similarly track item quantities by item type:

\\[ I\_{t+1}^{(k)}=I_t^{(k)}+P_t^{(k)}-C_t^{(k)} \\]

Where production \\(P\\) includes gathering, drops, shop issuance, and crafting output; consumption \\(C\\) includes crafting inputs, actual use, contract delivery, and despawning.

I'd log the following metrics during playtests:

| Metric                                | Why it matters                             |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Total circulating gold                | Detect inflation or excessive scarcity     |
| Median player wealth                  | Measure typical economic progression       |
| Gold earned per minute by activity    | Compare professions and strategies         |
| Share of gold held by richest players | Detect runaway snowballing                 |
| Market stockouts                      | Identify shortages and broken supply loops |
| Contract completion rate              | Determine whether contracts are viable     |
| Average cargo loss per death          | Tune economic risk                         |
| Time to first Charter                 | Balance the economic victory route         |

These logs would help us tune the economy empirically, rather than guessing prices and rewards.

# 8. Important exploits and how to prevent them

There are several problems I'd anticipate early.

| Potential exploit                                           | Mitigation                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Buy from official shop, sell elsewhere for unlimited profit | Finite regional demand, inventory, and purchasing liquidity |
| Repeatedly craft an item for guaranteed profit              | Input costs, workshop fees, bounded purchase orders         |
| Farm defenseless civilians endlessly                        | Limited loot, negligible combat XP, legal penalties         |
| Repeatedly kill cooperating players for gold                | PvP primarily transfers existing wealth                     |
| Bank all wealth and trade risk-free                         | Goods remain physically carried and vulnerable              |
| Monopolize essential items                                  | Official shops guarantee basic survival equipment           |
| Spam trivial contracts to gain Charter access               | Endorsements require substantial, distinct commissions      |
| Hoard goods to prevent everyone else's progress             | Multiple sources, substitutes, and replenishment            |

I particularly want to avoid making killing ordinary civilians more profitable than using the intended economic systems.

Civilian NPCs are important to the game's stealth atmosphere. They shouldn't simply become walking gold farms.

# 9. What I'd implement for the first playable economic prototype

I would keep the initial scope deliberately small.

## Economy vertical slice

# 12–15

Economic commodities

Raw, intermediate, and finished

# 5–8

Crafting recipes

Short transformation chains

# 4

Contract archetypes

Supply, procurement, crafting, courier

Supporting systems

- One official store and bank per city
- Unified weight-limited inventory
- Cargo drops and gold penalties on death
- Simple NPC supply/demand ticks
- One market-disrupting world event
- Sovereign Charter purchasing and activation

The test should determine whether players can discover and execute multiple profitable strategies without repeatedly following the same route.

If three markets and a dozen goods are already fun, we can scale the map and product variety.

If they aren't, adding fifty more commodities probably won't fix the fundamental gameplay.

## 10. My proposed decisions to lock

I'd tentatively adopt these rules for the next specification.

| Design question  | Proposed decision                                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Inventory        | Unified, weight-limited, shared with combat                  |
| Death            | Carried commodities drop; equipment and progression persist  |
| Gold security    | Protected deposits at physical official banks                |
| Insurance        | Postpone                                                     |
| Escorts          | Simple paid NPC guards and optional player cooperation       |
| Market           | Finite commodity stock, purchasing liquidity, bounded prices |
| NPC economy      | Abstract production/consumption with visible local activity  |
| Crafting         | Short recipes and fixed workshops                            |
| Contracts        | Mix of repeatable jobs and limited lucrative commissions     |
| Gold supply      | PvE, contracts, NPC purchases, events                        |
| Gold sinks       | Official shops, services, fees, penalties, Charter           |
| Economic victory | Charter temporarily opens a contestable Grand Heart route    |

My one remaining concern is the relationship between cargo security and assassination.

How much of a merchant's accumulated value should actually be at risk when traveling?

If only commodities can be stolen, players might convert wealth into banked gold as quickly as possible, making piracy less attractive. If most assets are lootable, economic players could lose an hour of progress to one ambush.

I lean toward the middle: protect banked wealth and equipped gear, make carried commodities fully lootable, and let players choose their risk exposure through how much cargo and cash they carry.

That preserves the fun of economic strategy while giving assassins and thieves meaningful opportunities.
